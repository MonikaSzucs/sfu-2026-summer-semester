Circularity and the circular economy can be expressed at many different levels, including the individual product level, organizational and inter-organizational levels, and system level.

The goal of keeping resources in the system for as long as possible is at the heart of the circular economy. Two critical ways of achieving this are by: (1) giving new life to resources and (2) extending the life of resources. Let’s look at each of these in turn.

Giving new life to resources
The circular economy is often associated with the idea of “closing the loop,” where the otherwise linear model or lifecycle of something (e.g., a product) circles back and flows again into the starting point. That is, rather than resources following a cradle-to-grave route where they one day simply end up in a landfill or incinerator, they follow a cradle-to-cradle route where they reach the end of the lifecycle in their current use and then one day become the raw materials for something else.

Giving resources new life by connecting the end of a lifecycle to the beginning of another lifecycle is what closing the loop looks like, and is an important part of the circular economy.

A closed loop can result in the creation of a new version of the same object (e.g., a product) or the creation of something entirely different (e.g., a different unrelated product).

[Explaining the Circular Economy and How Society Can Re-think Progress | Animated Video Essay](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zCRKvDyyHmI)


Closing the loop also doesn’t merely have to happen at the end of a product’s lifecyle. It can also happen with the raw materials or any other resources being used throughout the lifecycle such as the manufacturing or processing stage.

It is helpful to think about opportunities for creating a closed loop via post-consumer and post-industrial waste.

Post-industrial waste is often easier to use because it has gone through fewer stages of processing. Post-consumer waste can be difficult to use as the raw materials for something else because it often exists as highly processed blends of raw materials.

Decisions at the design stage can have important implications for the feasibility of giving a product a new life and closing the loop. For example, synthetic blended fabrics used for clothes are much more difficult to separate and reuse than pure fabrics (e.g., 100% cotton).

Extending the life of resources
Prolonging the lifespan of resources is an important part of both linear and circular systems and a powerful way to have a positive impact. This entails making decisions that allow resources or a product to stay in its use or reuse stage for as long as possible, or to extend its life for as long as possible.

One important way to extend the life of something is through maintenance and repairs. While having products that can be repaired and having businesses that support such repairs and maintenance is one of the most straightforward ways we can extend the life of something, unfortunately, in today's society getting something repaired is not always easy or even possible.

Many businesses often deliberately take steps to prevent repairs, especially from third parties or everyday people, and approach their product manufacturing in such a way that it's actually just easier and cheaper to buy an entirely new item.

Today we're starting to see some movement towards businesses supporting repairs and maintenance. We are also seeing some businesses capitalizing on the market opportunity to support seamless product upgrades without having to purchase entirely new products.

### Forced labour and modern slavery
Modern slavery is an umbrella term that generally describes extreme forms of labour exploitation in which a person cannot refuse work or leave because of threats, violence, coercion, deception, and/or abuse of power. It includes forced labour (e.g., bonded labour, slavery, human trafficking), and it concerns all demographics.

While global estimates are rough, the International Labour Organization reports that 27.6 million people are in situations of forced labour globally and that 63% of such forced labour takes place in the private sector, often in the hidden corners and sub-tiers of supply chains, which can make it difficult to detect the issue (ILO, n.d.Links to an external site.).

What makes forced labour and modern slavery particularly tricky is that perpetrators often take elaborate steps to hide the human rights violation. Over the years, the market for audit services focused on forced labour and modern slavery has also grown around the world. In addition to the emergence of new specialized audit firms and certifications, traditional auditors have expanded to the domain of social audits. While such audits, on the surface, are intended to help detect the presence of forced labour and modern slavery as well as other human rights violations, there have been notable challenges. These include:

- Financial conflicts of interest
 - Auditors are paid to certify that these exploitative practices aren’t taking place.

- Institutional design
 - Supply chain tiers that are more likely to have human exploitation tend not to be audited.


- Methods of audits
 - Suppliers and workplaces know in advance that auditors are coming and can prepare.
 - Multi companies are requesting individual audits of the same facilities, which is creating “audit fatigue” as some facilities are extremely occupied with audits.
 - Those conducting auditors often lack experience and domain expertise.

Some entities have noted these challenges and have taken the initiative to address them. For example, to address the issue of audit fatigue, the Social & Labor Convergence Program was created to promote the sharing of data from assessments amongst buyers that would normally request their own proprietary audits.

#### What happens when you detect forced labour and modern slavery?
When a company is linked to forced labour and modern slavery, it often make its into the headlines of major media outlets. While one common response is to cut ties with suppliers where the issue is taking place, leading companies have recognized the limitation of such an approach.

Cutting ties with a perpetrator of forced labour and modern slavery does not address the issue. In today’s global economy, there is no shortage of demand. The perpetrator will simply start supplying another buyer.

Addressing forced labour and modern slavery requires notable work to address the root causes of the issue and development mechanisms. While we have previously looked at the potential of worker-driven solutions, we can learn a lot about what it takes to address forced labour and modern slavery long-term from Patagonia.
