Module Intro
Social Foundations and Floor
The social and people side of sustainability is very much overlooked. But
sustainability really inherently deals with meeting the needs of people and humanity
as businesses and organizations. We need to remember that our ability to exist and
survive, let alone thrive, depends on people in and around our organizations and
value chains.
While we're seeing a lot of social unrest and changing dynamics around the world, in
the big picture, we are seeing more movement towards expecting and even
mandating organizations and businesses to have a positive contribution to society
and contribute to the rights and wellbeing of the workers and communities to which
they are connected.

Sustainability is concerned with both environmental and social issues. An important, but often overlooked, aspect of business sustainability is understanding that there is a social floor consisting of what some call the social foundations.

A social floor is a lower threshold or limit of society and humanity that we must not fall short of to ensure a level of social well-being where no one falls short of life’s essentials.

The social foundations are 12 dimensions that represent the minimum standards aligned with the sustainable development goals:

- Health
- Food
- Water
- Energy
- Gender equality
- Social equity
- Political voice
- Peace and justice
- Income and work
- Education
- Networks

The social foundations make up the other boundary depicted in the doughnut economics model.

It may seem obvious that the rights and well-being of workers has an impact on the health of organizations and that organizations should care about it, but it is surprisingly often overlooked. However, expectations and behaviours are changing.

Importantly, ensuring the rights and well-being of workers is more than providing perks. It’s about taking action to address the root cause of issues, rather than provide individual perks that may offer surface level remedies.

 
“One important drawback of an individually focused approach is that it often fails to address systemic issues. When workers within and connected to an organization thrive, the organization is more likely to thrive. By ensuring the rights and well-being of workers, organizations can minimize risks and disruptions and create workforces that allow both people and organizations to thrive.

Research and society increasingly show that the rights and well-being of all workers connected to an organization matter and have impact. This includes employees, contract workers, and workers throughout a supply chain.

The rights and well-being (including resilience) of communities is important to organizations.

 
“Community resilience describes a community’s ability to respond and adapt to acute shocks and long-term stresses.”
— Dekker, 2020, p.6Links to an external site.
Organizations are connected to a multitude of communities directly and indirected linked to their operations, from the communities in which they operate and/or are linked to through their supply chains to the consumer communities that they serve and many others.

“Communities are made up of and are part of intricate environmental, social, and economic systems, which means that building community resilience needs to be approached from a systems perspective.”

Community health (e.g., rights and well-being) and resilience matter for many reasons:

Risk management
- Community relations
- Collaboration
- Systems influence and social acceptance

Remember though, that workers and communities are unique, and as organizations, it’s important to take the time to engage with relevant worker groups and communities to better understand their individual needs.

Nonetheless, societal expectations have changed. Not too long ago, it was highly contested whether or not businesses should be thinking about contributing to various parts of society. Nowadays, it is recognized that businesses have a key role to play in contributing to societal well-being and that the well-being of society has important impacts on business.

Geographic Communities
The health of geographic communities in which organizations operate can have a big impact on day to day operations. Strong community well-being creates a strong environment for business to thrive. Take for example the following:

- Limited access to education or water directly affects businesses employees and the workforce.
- Affordable housing / living wages affect the potential workforce – that is, who lives in the community or who leaves the community (“brain drain”).

> The Calls to Action directly related to business
Understanding that Call to Action #92 directly relates to “Business and Reconciliation" is key to knowing how businesses have a role in reconciliation.

92. We call upon the corporate sector in Canada to adopt the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples as a reconciliation framework and to apply its principles, norms, and standards to corporate policy and core operational activities involving Indigenous peoples and their lands and resources. This would include, but not be limited to, the following:

- Commit to meaningful consultation, building respectful relationships, and obtaining the free, prior, and informed consent of Indigenous peoples before proceeding with economic development projects.
- Ensure that Aboriginal peoples have equitable access to jobs, training, and education opportunities in the corporate sector, and that Aboriginal communities gain long-term sustainable benefits from economic development projects.
- Provide education for management and staff on the history of Aboriginal peoples, including the history and legacy of residential schools, the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, Treaties and Aboriginal rights, Indigenous law, and Aboriginal–Crown relations. This will require skills based training in intercultural competency, conflict resolution, human rights, and anti-racism.

The United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP) offers a detailed framework to follow and when it comes to business, it specifically states that businesses should:

- Adopt and implement a formal policy addressing Indigenous peoples’ rights and committing the business to respect Indigenous peoples’ rights.
- Conduct human rights due diligence to assess actual or potential adverse impacts on indigenous peoples’ rights, integrate findings and take action, track and communicate externally on performance.
- Consult in good faith with Indigenous peoples in relation to all matters that may affect them or their rights.
- Commit to obtain (and maintain) the free, prior and informed consent of Indigenous peoples for projects that affect their rights, in line with the spirit of the UN Declaration.
- Establish or cooperate through legitimate processes to remediate any adverse impacts on Indigenous peoples’ rights.
- Establish or cooperate with an effective and culturally appropriate grievance mechanism.

- Competition for local small stores (e.g., cafes)
