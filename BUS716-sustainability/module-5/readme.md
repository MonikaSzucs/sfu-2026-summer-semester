## Lifecycle Thinking: Lifecycle Mapping and Assessments
00:00 - 00:36
Sustainability relates to both the social and environmental aspects of a business. As
businesses and economic systems overall are embedded within the broader social
and environmental systems that make up society and the planet. Lifecycle thinking
really is a concrete idea and practice that, if done correctly, takes into
consideration both the social and environmental impacts of a business. And really,
at the end of the day, embraces the idea of systems thinking.
00:36 - 01:57
As a practice, Lifecycle thinking offers a structured way to consider the
environmental and social impacts of a business throughout its entire value chain,
from what some people call cradle to grave. What's really great is that this idea can
be applied to products, services, processes, and really anything else related to a
business or organization. The real beauty of lifecycle thinking, however, is that it can
be applied as a conceptual and qualitative tool or a calculative and quantitative tool.
In resource constrained environments, or when you just don't have too much time,
it's very manageable to embrace lifecycle thinking as a conceptual mapping tool or
exercise to identify impacts, risks, and opportunities. When you do have time and
resources, however, it can very much be embraced as a comprehensive, calculative
assessment or exercise. In fact, in many cases, lifecycle thinking tends to take place
as a formal lifecycle assessment or analysis that involves hundreds of hours, highly
trained professionals conducting a very comprehensive assessment based on
standards laid out by organizations such as the International Organization for
standardization or what we know as ISO.
1
01:57 - 01:57
In any business or society, you may encounter people that embrace this idea of
lifecycle thinking quite naturally. They automatically think about the social and
environmental impacts of everything throughout its entire life. But more and more,
you might find yourself embracing or encountering people that embrace the idea of
lifecycle thinking through the increasingly common practice of conducting a
lifecycle assessment or lifecycle analyses, terms that we use interchangeably, but
more commonly just call an LCA for short. In disciplines such as environmental
sciences and engineering, the idea of lifecycle thinking and more commonly
lifecycle assessments and analyses, have long been an embraced practice. What's
important to recognize, though, is that in these disciplines and really in the world,
the primary focus has tended to be on environmental impacts. When we engage in
lifecycle thinking, whether that's a conceptual mapping exercise or a formal
lifecycle assessment, we want to be broadening our scope to consider the social
impacts of whatever we're analyzing, because we know that business sustainability
really relates to both social and environmental factors.

Lifecycle thinking is a concrete practice that offers a structured way to consider environmental and social impacts across your value chain, from “cradle to grave,” and can be applied to products, services, and processes.

 
"A value chain refers to the full lifecycle of a product or process, including material sourcing, production, consumption and disposal/recycling processes.”
— (WBCSD, 2011, p.3Links to an external site.)
Lifecycle thinking is very adaptable. It can be used as a qualitative and conceptual tool as well as a quantitative and calculative tool, as outlined in the table below.

| Lifecycle Mapping | Lifecycle Assessments / Analysis (LCA) |
| -- | -- |
| Qualtitative and conceptual| Quantiative and calculative |
| Manageable with limitated resources/time | Can require extensive resources/time |
| helpful to idtendit potential risks and opportunities | helpful to quantify impacts |
| Can be embraced by anyone | Usually conducted by trained professional |

Official lifecycle assessments or analyses (LCAs) have long been embraced in disciplines such as environmental sciences and engineering. When applied in industry, LCAs sometimes follow standards such as those set out by the International Organization for Standardization (ISO)Links to an external site..

Historically, lifecycle thinking has been embraced through an environmental lens. Today, however, it is critical to broaden the scope to include both social and environmental considerations, particularly because businesses are embedded in social and environmental systems.

![](/BUS716-sustainability/module-5/img/companies_embedded_in_and_responsive_to_their_environmental_and_Social_context.png)

Lifecycle thinking is anchored in systems thinking. It looks beyond the immediacy of something and looks at impact through all stages of the lifecycle, effectively examining impact across time and in different places.

## Steps of Lifecycle Mapping
00:00 - 00:22
There are many different ways to conduct a lifecycle assessment or a conceptual
mapping exercise. But what I, experts, and practitioners have really found to be
helpful is to really approach things systematically. Where you follow a series of
steps to really make sure that you don't miss anything.
00:22 - 00:59
Overall, there are three things that I really like to stress when it comes to embracing
lifecycle thinking on the ground. First, really take the time upfront to clearly specify
the functional unit at hand and the system boundaries of whatever you're analyzing.
This is especially important when you're conducting lifecycle assessments or
conceptual maps with other people, because if you don't have clarity on what you're
actually analyzing and the parameters or boundaries around it, including things such
as geography, you might actually end up analyzing slightly different things, which
makes integrating findings a little bit dicult.
01:00 - 01:34
Next, really do proceed through things systematically to ensure that you don't miss
anything. Map out all the different stages of the lifecycle. Then do multiple passes
through it with different lenses or different foci focus on the environmental
impacts, and then switch to the social impacts. Within each of these, drill down
even further and focus on very specific issues within these broader categories. This
really helps make sure that you aren't missing any entire category or important thing
related to your product, service, or process.
1
01:35 - 02:18
Lastly, when it comes to identifying interventions and deciding on potential action
plans, take the time to understand local conditions and potential constraints.
Sometimes the most straightforward interventions that could have a fantastic
impact have not been embraced for very specific reasons, such as local norms or
regulations that really stand in their way. Understand such circumstances and
consider what really is realistic within a given context and time span, and also
understand how pursuing one intervention might actually entail additional work
elsewhere to create a more welcoming environment for that focal intervention.
02:19 - 03:23
One example that I like to use is the simple act of suggesting that people hang dry
their clothes to improve the environmental impact of clothes. We know that the use
and the reuse stages of clothes through washing and drying clothes is where a
major part of the environmental impact happens. But what many people don't
realize is that in many buildings, neighborhoods, and entire municipalities, Hang
drying clothes is actually a banned practice. You're not allowed to do it. And in many
cases this is purely for aesthetics reasons. Places deemed hang drying clothes as
not being aesthetically pleasing and has the potential to drive down the value of
property and neighboring properties. So when considering any intervention, really
do take the time to think things through. And remember, when considering any
type of intervention, think about the unintended and intended consequences, and
really consider whether or not you're actually improving things overall, or merely
shifting the burden and impact elsewhere.

> step 1 Determine your functional unit and your system boundaries
Be clear about what you are analyzing and the parameters surrounding it, particularly if you are mapping out a lifecycle as a team. If you don’t have clarity with regards to the functional unit and the system boundaries, you and your teammates might end up analyzing slightly different things.

A functional unit is “your unit of analysis – it describes the product, service, or system whose impacts are being calculated. When selecting functional units, the aim is to choose a unit that allows for fair comparisons between your product or service and other alternatives that could serve the same purpose.”

- Identify a “function” (e.g., be warm, interact with people).
- Ensure it is comparable to alternatives.
- Be as specific as possible
- Choose your concrete item (e.g., a 100% cotton shirt).

System boundaries are the parameters and conditions surrounding your product and analysis. Two main system boundaries are (i) scope (e.g., how far do you extend your analysis?), and (ii) geography (e.g., where will each stage occur?)

- Scope: How far will you extend your analysis?
 - Direct and/or indirect impacts?
 - More details = more accurate, but be pragmatic.
 - Specify scope: What part of the product are you analyzing?
- Geography: Where will stages occur?
 - Identify locations of each stage/component of your functional unit.

> Step 2. Map the key stages in the lifecycle of the product

The lifecycle of products entails the generic stages set out in the visualization below. While there may be sub-stages (e.g., multiple stages of processing) for any given product, it is helpful and recommended to start with the generic stages of the lifecycle and modify things as needed

![](/BUS716-sustainability/module-5/img/stages_in_the_lifecycle_of_a_product.png)

Because lifecycling thinking really entails considering the impacts of something beyond a single stage (e.g., what a single organizational entity might control), it is imperative to give dedicated attention to each stage of a product's lifecycle so you do not miss anything.

- Raw material extraction and processing.
- Component manufacturing, assembly, and packaging.
- Distribution and sales.
- Use, maintenance, and reuse.
- Disassembly, repurposing, recycling, composting, disposal.
- Transportation.

It's really helpful to use these basic stages as your starting point. Think of it as the canvas that you're going to start to fill out and modify as needed. What's crucial, however, is that you give actual attention to each of these different stages of the lifecycle and don't gloss over them which can be a common mistake.

> Step 6. Develop an action plan

With your interventions and individual circumstances (e.g., resources, goals, local conditions) in mind, it is now time to prioritize and decide on an action plan.

Think about what work is needed for strategies to be adopted:

- More research on the impacts?
- Identification of alternatives?
- Calculating feasibility?
- Securing resources and expertise to make a shift?

Gauge your realistic ability to influence change:

- How much power / control do we have?

Note that there is no “one best way” to prioritize in a lifecycle mapping exercise, but consider the following:

- Where could we make the biggest impact?
- What is in our direct control?
- Where do we have influence?
- Which issues present a reputational risk to our business?
- Where could we differentiate our product?
- Which can be done easily, and which would require longer term investments?
- What information do we need to proceed?

Decide which factors you will use to guide your decision-making and identify initiatives to prioritize as well as the actions that you need to take to make them happen.

It’s important to think about the big picture. Think about if you are improving the impact of your product overall or simply shifting the burden elsewhere or creating new issues.

It is also critical to be mindful of local conditions and constraints. Action plans should be realistic. Sometimes the most straightforward interventions and potentially impactful ones haven’t been embraced because of key constraints that make them difficult to implement (e.g., local norms, policies/regulations).

## Relevance of Lifecycle Thinking
Overall, the embracement of life cycle thinking, involving both the social and
environmental aspects is really helpful and important for many different reasons.
First, it's increasingly being regulated. Second, there's increasing social
expectations and demands regarding impact. Things are changing in the business
world and society, and while not everyone cares about impact, many people do.
Third, it allows businesses, customers, governments and other stakeholders to
really make more informed decisions with regards to purchases. And lastly, it can be
a helpful way to identify opportunities for savings, improving impact and
eciencies, and minimizing risks.

While some businesses and people embrace lifecycle thinking for impact or moral reasons (e.g., they are motivated to understand the big picture impact of something and how they can improve it), there are several reasons that lifecycle thinking is used in businesses today.

1. Regulations concerning various parts of the lifecycle are increasingly common.
2. Societal expectations, demands, or concerns regarding impact are increasing.
3. Allow businesses, customers, governments, and other stakeholders to make more informed decisions.
4. Opportunities for savings, improving efficiencies and impacts, and minimizing risks.

Among the many reasons for embracing lifecycle thinking, regulations or legislation in jurisdictions around the world is an increasingly important one, particularly because they illustrate how businesses may need to be considering or are affected by the broader impacts of their products, services, or processes regardless of their positions towards them.

In recent years, jurisdictions around the world have adopted legislation concerning forced labour, modern slavery, and human rights violations more broadly in supply chains. This is a particularly important development because such legislation is a concrete example of how expectations and requirements have shifted beyond the immediacy of businesses’ direct operations and environmental issues to now include social issues in supply chains and beyond.

To date, the bulk of such legislation has taken the form of transparency and disclosure legislation in which a variety of organizations, not just for-profit companies, from across sectors need to prepare annual statements or reports concerning issues such as forced labour, modern slavery, and other human rights-related issues in their supply chains. This is an important detail because such legislation does not require organizations to take action to address such issues.

## Planned Obsolescence

00:00 - 00:25
The regulation of the early stages of a life cycle and upstream impacts, such as
human rights violations in a supply chain, are important recent developments. But
it's really important to be aware that other parts of the life cycle are also being
regulated beyond traditionally regulated things, such as the disposal of harmful
waste in the environment.
00:25 - 01:11
For example, in today's highly capitalistic society, based on high amounts of
consumerism, people are increasingly aware of and concerned with this practice of
planned obsolescence, where companies intentionally plan for their products to
become obsolete, deliberately shortening the lifespan of their products to
encourage people to ultimately buy new ones. Apple, for example, is infamous for
this. They have been known to deliberately slow their phones, laptops and other
products to encourage you to get the most recent releases. In recent years,
however, we have really started to see such practices being confronted and
companies start to be held accountable for their actions.


Yet, lifecycle thinking isn’t only relevant to businesses in terms of upstream impacts. Take, for example, the idea of planned obsolescence, which is “a situation in which goods are deliberately made or designed so that they do not last for a long period of time” - Cambridge Dictionary

The idea of planned obsolescence has long been embraced as a strategy by businesses. They intentionally make their products in a way so they will become obsolete one day to encourage the purchase of new ones.

Businesses in key industries (e.g., fast fashion) also embrace the idea of perceived obsolescence where a product may still be functional, but you are made to believe that it is no longer needed or relevant.

Many businesses deliberately shorten the use-stage of a product's lifecycle. However, society has taken note and started to hold businesses accountable for such actions.

For example, Apple has faced class action lawsuits associated with what the media has called “Batterygate”Links to an external site. and their intentional slowing down of their smart phones.

[Life Cycle of a Smartphone](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YDOu_bXaEoY)


Did you know? Planned obsolescence has a long history and has even been traced back to the early years of the lightbulb? In the 1920s, the infamous “Phoebus cartel” that comprised the major lightbulb manufacturers that controlled the market colluded to shorten the lifespan of lightbulbs.

[Planned Obsolescence Sucks. Here's Why It Still Exists.](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wzWU7D0S9_8)

## Closing Thoughts
00:00 - 00:29
Lifecycle thinking and practices such as lifecycle assessments and conceptual
mapping exercises, really are a way of the future and really do embrace the idea of
systems thinking. Regulation, social expectations are increasingly pointing towards
businesses needing to be accountable for the impacts of their products, services
and processes throughout their entire life cycles.
00:29 - 00:53
What's great is that there are many different examples of companies embracing
lifecycle thinking and in many cases, conducting and publishing formal lifecycle
assessments, and we can use these as models and inspiration. At the end of the
day, sustainability relates to both social and environmental considerations, and
lifecycle thinking really is a systematic and concrete way for us to start to consider
these.

Lifecycle thinking is a concrete practice anchored on systems thinking that is increasingly embraced in businesses for an array of reasons. As a practice, it can help us integrate many of the different aspects of sustainability that have been overlooked by many throughout modern history and can be used as a helpful tool for identifying how we can improve the impacts of businesses overall in the pursuit of sustainability.

Whether it takes the form of lifecycle mapping exercises or formal lifecycle assessments, lifecycle thinking offers us a systematic way to consider the social and environmental impacts of businesses from cradle to grave.
