This week, two things really stuck with me.

First, the breakdown of objective, subjective, and inter-subjective truth helps explain why culture feels so hard to change. A shared narrative isn't just someone's opinion—it becomes "the way things are done here." That's why new behaviours have to come before new meanings. You can't talk someone out of a culture; you have to act differently together, over and over, until the story shifts.

Second, polarity management changed how I see culture. It's not a fixed set of traits but consistent choices among dilemmas. We're trained to think OR—decentralize OR centralize—but that's a trap. The real work is AND: how do we get both? The video example about self-assured vs. humble leadership hit home. Focus only on confidence and you become arrogant; only on humility and you seem spineless. You don't resolve the tension—you leverage it.

That said, I'm still wondering: how do you tell the difference between a healthy polarity and a simple contradiction that just needs to be fixed? Not every tension is a useful paradox. Some are just bad strategy.



Module 3

Based on the materials you shared for Module 3, the thing that struck me most is the inherent tension within the “Environmental Grid” and the lifecycle diagram showing how businesses create the environment they then must adapt to.

I find it fascinating—and slightly counterintuitive—that the most nurturing environment for long-term success isn’t one with low uncertainty or abundant resources, but one with medium resource scarcity and medium information complexity (the top-right quadrant of the grid). Your materials note that organizations landing there face pressure for both efficiency and innovation, but not so much challenge that they fail.

What really drives the point home is the industry lifecycle path: a successful new technology attracts so many entrants that capital becomes scarce and complexity spikes, leading to a “shakeout.” The survivors aren’t necessarily the first movers, but those who land in that medium-medium zone afterward.

This reframes “uncertainty” as not just a risk to mitigate, but a strategic design driver. It also explains why functional structures (efficiency-focused) fail in dynamic environments, while adhocracies or matrix designs (innovation-focused) struggle when resource scarcity is low but complexity is high.

For PMC’s case, this suggests their organization design must match where they are on that path—not just their current size or product type.

Module 4
What struck me in this week’s materials is the tension between Semco’s trust-based approach and Holocracy’s rule-based one. Both reject traditional hierarchy, but they design power very differently.

From my perspective, Semco feels more aligned with how power actually works in relationships. There’s no rulebook—just transparency, voting, common sense, and units under 150 people. Power emerges through stewardship and results, as Bushe describes. Holocracy, however, surprised me. Despite claiming to be self-organizing, its constitution and “non-objection” processes feel bureaucratic. I worry that codifying empowerment so tightly might create new forms of coercion rather than liberation.

Morgan’s chapter on power reminded me that all designs embed politics. If I were advising iQmetrix, I would caution against adopting Holocracy unless their people genuinely want high process discipline. The risk is losing Tannenbaum’s “total power”—the feeling everyone can get things done. Semco’s looser model seems more likely to increase that.


Module 5
What struck me most this week is the gap between ADKAR's neat five-step model and what actually made Keda successful.

ADKAR assumes change happens linearly: awareness leads to desire, then knowledge, ability, reinforcement. But Keda succeeded because they did things the model barely mentions—like keeping senior leaders visibly engaged, letting unions help design the rollout, and running pilots so people could try before committing.

I also struggle with how ADKAR frames resistance as a deficit in the person (they lack awareness or desire). The Keda case shows people resist for rational reasons: poor training, broken systems, or fear of the unknown. Bridges' "Neutral Zone" explains this better—people need time to let go of the old before embracing the new.

The real insight for me: change management models work best as conversation starters, not checklists. Keda followed the spirit of ADKAR and Kotter's steps, but their success came from adapting to real people's emotions and local politics. That's harder to teach, but more important than memorizing any model.

Module 6

What stood out to me this week is the difference between the Diagnostic approach and the Dialogic approach. I always thought change management meant you had to diagnose the problem first, come up with a vision, then roll it out. But the Dialogic OD stuff turns that on its head—no diagnosis, no leadership vision upfront? That feels risky, but the examples (like Roadway or the IKEA Future Search) make it seem like it works.

The part that clicked for me was about "generative images." The example with miners—changing "safety compliance" to "easy safety"—is so simple but powerful. It’s not about convincing people with more arguments; it’s about giving them a new way to see the problem. That feels more realistic than trying to force new procedures on people who are resistant.

I’m still trying to wrap my head around how you'd know when to use Diagnostic vs. Dialogic. The Heifetz stuff about technical vs. adaptive problems helps, but in real life, problems feel messy and mixed. I feel like in the Jet Blue case (which I haven't fully read yet) there’s probably moments where leaders had to hold back and let emergence happen, and moments where they just needed to make a call.


Module 7

What’s really forcing me to rethink leadership is the slide asking why generative change is still so uncommon after 30 years of success.

I realize I’ve been taught to expect leaders to have a vision. But Snowden, Boone, and Bushe are saying that in complex situations, a leader who claims to know the answer is actually the problem. That’s hard to swallow.

What’s forcing me to sit with this is the lockdown example. I saw a large retailer pivot in weeks when normally they’d take a year. They didn’t have a plan. They had a purpose (“keep people safe, get essentials out”) and trusted store teams to figure out curbside pickup, scheduling, even plexiglass placement. No central command knew best.

So if that works in a crisis, why don’t we do it all the time? Because it’s scary. Leaders fear looking weak. Employees fear chaos. And honestly? I’d probably panic too if my boss said “I don’t know, let’s find out together.”

What’s forcing me is realizing my own comfort with certainty might be the real barrier, not the model itself.
