# Quiz 2

1. Launch Strategy (Session 4)

An established online retailer with millions of existing customers decides to let outside merchants list and sell their own products through its site, taking a small commission on each sale. This move sidesteps the chicken-and-egg problem because it is an example of:
- The follow-the-rabbit strategy — converting an existing pipeline business into a platform

2. Launch Strategy (Session 4)

Before expanding a campus-focused social app nationally, the founders first got nearly every resident of a single dorm onto the platform, making sure it delivered real value within that one dorm before trying to grow further. This illustrates:
- The atomic network — the smallest viable network that has to work on its own before you scale

3. Launch Strategy (Session 4)

A new ride-hailing startup has a limited launch budget and has to decide where to spend it: subsidizing drivers so wait times stay low, or subsidizing riders so demand looks strong. Choosing to spend most of the budget on the scarcer, harder-to-attract side first reflects:
- The pricing asymmetry principle — subsidize the side that is scarcer or more price-sensitive to reach critical mass

4. Monetization (Session 5)

A freight-matching marketplace charges shippers nothing upfront but takes 8% of the value of every completed shipment booked through the platform. This pricing approach is best classified as a:

- Transaction fee model

5. Monetization (Session 5)

A deals platform takes a 50% cut from small local businesses on every discount voucher redeemed, while consumers pay nothing and are courted with ever-bigger discounts. Merchants increasingly stop renewing deals because the math no longer works for them. This illustrates:
- Taxing the wrong side — extracting value from the side that can least absorb it, undermining the network over time

6. Monetization (Session 5)

A professional networking platform lets almost anyone build a full profile and connect with others for free, then charges only power users — recruiters and salespeople — for premium search and messaging tools. This free tier primarily functions to:
- Build a moat by maximizing the size and density of the free network, which increases what the paid tier is worth

7. Monetization (Session 5)

A mobile game developer builds a large following inside a major app store, then tries to direct players to buy in-game currency on the developer's own website to avoid the store's 30% commission. The app store's terms of service block this practice. At its core, this dispute is about:
- Platform tax and pricing power — how much of a producer's revenue a platform can extract, and whether it can block producers from routing around that tax

8. Platform Metrics (Session 6)

A marketplace proudly reports $50M in "revenue" for the year, but that figure is actually the total value of goods that changed hands through the platform — most of which was paid out to sellers. Real company revenue turns out to be closer to $4M. This is an example of:
- The GMV trap — mistaking gross merchandise value (throughput) for real revenue

9. Platform Metrics (Session 6)

A B2B sourcing platform tracks two numbers: the share of buyer searches that return zero usable results, and the share of seller inquiries that go unanswered for more than 48 hours. Both numbers have been climbing. This rise most directly signals an increase in:
- Interaction failure rate

10. Platform Metrics (Session 6)

Before expanding into new international markets, a home-sharing platform invests heavily in identity verification, review systems, and a guarantee program — well before adding more listings in those markets. This investment treats:
- Trust as an operational metric that gates expansion, not a nice-to-have
